# Japanese Subsidies: Paying Up Front vs. Reimbursement — A Complete Cash-Flow Guide for Chinese Business Owners

If you are a Chinese business owner in Japan applying for a government subsidy, at some point you have surely wondered: **When will the subsidy arrive? Can my company get the money now? Or do I have to front the cost myself first?**

This question concerns not only cash-flow management but the very survival of the business. This article takes an in-depth look at the payment mechanism of Japanese subsidies, at ways to handle the pressure of paying up front, and at the pitfalls Chinese business owners easily fall into.

## The Payment Model for Japanese Subsidies: Paying Up Front Is the Norm

### The reality of paying up front: most programs require you to fund the project yourself

In Japan, **more than 90% of subsidies adopt the "後払い" (post-payment) model.** This means:

1. **The company first funds the project itself** to complete the implementation
2. **The company fronts all costs** (materials, labor, equipment, etc.)
3. **Only after the project is completed and inspected** can the company apply for reimbursement
4. **Submit complete supporting documents and invoices**
5. **Wait for government review** (usually two to four weeks)
6. **Only then do the funds reach the company's account**

For example, suppose you applied for a 5-million-yen equipment-purchase subsidy. The whole process is:

- Months 1–3: The company buys the equipment and pays 5 million yen
- Month 4: The project is completed and various inspection documents are prepared
- Month 5: The reimbursement application is submitted to the government
- Months 6–7: Government review
- Month 8: The funds arrive

**This means the company must front 5 million yen over eight months.**

### Exceptions: up-front payment and installment payment

Although rare, some subsidy programs do offer up-front payment:

**先払い (up-front payment)**
- Applicable programs: large infrastructure projects, emergency reconstruction subsidies
- Payment ratio: usually 30–50% of the total
- How to apply: submit an application to the managing body before the project starts
- Typical cases: the 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake reconstruction subsidy, Covid-19 emergency subsidies

**分割払い (installment payment)**
- Number of payments: usually three to five installments
- Payment trigger: by project-progress stage
- Example: a procurement subsidy might pay 30% at ordering, 40% at delivery, and 30% at inspection

## Specific Payment Timelines for Different Subsidies

### Subsidies for SMEs

Take Japan's most common **Monozukuri Subsidy** (manufacturing subsidy) as an example:

| Time point | Item | Amount fronted by company |
|---------|------|----------|
| January (Year 1) | Application deadline | 0 |
| March (Year 1) | Adoption announcement (results of selection published) | 0 |
| April–September (Year 1) | **Execution period** (company implements with its own funds) | **100% fronted** |
| October (Year 1) | Completion report submitted | 0 |
| November–December (Year 1) | Government inspection and review | 0 |
| January–February (Year 2) | Final subsidy payment | **Fronted funds recovered** |

**The key figure:** From application to receipt of funds, it takes an average of **13–15 months.**

### Sustainability Subsidy (Business Continuity Strengthening Plan)

This subsidy is paid relatively quickly:

- After passing the preliminary review: 50% paid (arrives within about two weeks)
- After completing inspection: the remaining 50% paid (arrives within about one month)
- **Total waiting time:** 6–9 months

### Employment Adjustment Subsidy (雇用調整助成金)

This is the fastest-paying type of subsidy:

- Application submission: can be applied for monthly
- **Payment cycle:** usually arrives 30–45 days after submission
- **No large up-front funding needed:** employee wages are paid by the company on time, and the subsidy goes directly into the company's account
- This is one of the few subsidies with **relatively low up-front-payment pressure**

## Specific Measures to Handle Up-Front-Payment Pressure

### Option 1: Financing supplement — linking bank loans with the subsidy

**The most effective cash-flow method is to use the subsidy as loan collateral.**

Many Japanese banks offer **"subsidy-secured financing":**

**Process:**
1. Obtain the subsidy adoption notice
2. Submit this notice and the project plan to the bank
3. The bank approves a loan amount (usually 80–100% of the subsidy)
4. The company obtains the loan and immediately fronts the project costs
5. Once the subsidy arrives, use it directly to repay the bank loan

**A concrete case:**
A Chinese electronics company applied for an 8-million-yen manufacturing subsidy. It submitted an application to the **Japan Finance Corporation** (日本政策金融公庫) and was approved for 6 million yen in financing at a monthly interest rate of 0.9%. The company first used the loan to front the costs and repaid it after the subsidy arrived; the total interest cost was about 36,000 yen (calculated over six months).

**Recommended financial institutions:**
- Japan Finance Corporation: government-backed, low interest rates (0.7–1.2%)
- Shoko Chukin Bank: professional SME financing
- Regional banks (such as Bank of Yokohama, Bank of Kyoto): friendly to local businesses

### Option 2: Deferred-payment agreements with suppliers

Many companies overlook the room to negotiate with suppliers.

**Negotiable terms:**

| Original terms | Optimized terms | Effect of optimization |
|---------|----------|--------|
| Cash on delivery | Payment after the subsidy arrives | Extends the payment period by 4–6 months |
| Payment within 30 days | Payment in 60–90 days | Eases cash-flow pressure |
| Full payment | 20% deposit + 80% deferred payment | Reduces initial front-loading by 80% |

**Negotiation script (Chinese translation):**

> "Our project has been awarded a Japanese government subsidy (adoption certificate submitted), and we expect the subsidy to arrive in month X of year X. We hope to work with your company: could payment be scheduled according to the subsidy's payment cycle? We can provide the government notice as credit assurance."

In Japanese business culture, a government subsidy is proof of an **extremely high credit rating.** Most suppliers are willing to defer.

### Option 3: A phased-procurement strategy

Instead of purchasing all equipment at once, do it in stages:

**Example:**
- Phase 1 (April): Purchase core equipment, 2 million yen
- Phase 2 (June): Purchase auxiliary equipment, 1.5 million yen
- Phase 3 (August): Purchase supporting facilities, 1.5 million yen
- **Benefit:** the pressure of front-loading each phase is smaller, and you can keep accumulating operating funds while waiting for the subsidy

This approach is especially suited to situations where **the subsidy is paid slowly but the project has flexible implementation timing.**

### Option 4: Crowdfunding and equity financing

For newly established Chinese companies, you may consider:

- **Angel investment:** invite Japanese investors to participate in exchange for equity rather than debt
- **Crowdfunding platforms:** Japan has professional crowdfunding platforms such as Makuake and CAMPFIRE
- **Chinese investment networks:** Chinese chambers of commerce in Japan and China's chamber of commerce in Japan often hold financing-matchmaking events

This approach does not add to your debt burden, but it involves equity dilution.

## Comparison of Up-Front-Payment Timelines Across Subsidy Types

### Fast-turnaround type (short front-loading period)

1. **Employment Adjustment Subsidy** (雇用調整助成金)
   - Front-loading period: 30–45 days
   - Reason: employee wages are a rigid expense, and the government understands the pressure on companies
   - Risk: low

2. **Business Recovery Subsidy** (事業復興補助金)
   - Front-loading period: 2–3 months
   - Reason: aimed at disaster-affected companies, with policy favor
   - Risk: low

### Medium-turnaround type (medium front-loading period)

3. **Management Innovation Subsidy** (経営革新補助金)
   - Front-loading period: 6–9 months
   - Installment payment: commonly a 50-50 split
   - Risk: medium

4. **Sustainability Subsidy** (持続化補助金)
   - Front-loading period: 6–8 months
   - Installment payment: 50% in the initial phase
   - Risk: medium

### Long-turnaround type (long front-loading period)

5. **Monozukuri Subsidy** (manufacturing subsidy)
   - Front-loading period: 13–15 months
   - Reason: large sums, strict review
   - Risk: high
   - **Response:** you must plan financing in advance

6. **R&D subsidy**
   - Front-loading period: 12–18 months
   - Reason: highly technical, complex inspection
   - Risk: high
   - **Response:** requires ample company-owned funds

## Cash-Flow Risk Management Checklist

### The 5 preparations you must make before applying

**1. Cash-flow forecast table**
Create a detailed monthly forecast, calculating the cash gap for each month:

```
Amount fronted in month 1: 5 million yen
Company operating expenses that month: 1 million yen
Sales revenue that month: 800,000 yen
Net cash consumption that month: 5.2 million yen
```

If the company account does not have 5.2 million yen, you must arrange financing in advance.

**2. Confirming your credit line**
- Bank loan line: ? yen
- Company's own funds: ? yen
- Total available funds: ? yen
- **Must be ≥ the amount of subsidy to be fronted**

**3. Confirming supplier payment terms**
Confirm one by one with each supplier:
- 20% deposit (at signing)
- 50% interim payment (at delivery)
- 30% final payment (after inspection, when the subsidy arrives)

**4. Subsidy risk assessment**
Although government subsidies are usually very stable, you still need to consider:
- Adoption probability: what is the average adoption rate for this program?
- Execution risk: can the project be completed on time?
- Policy risk: will this subsidy program continue in the future?

**5. Financing cost calculation**
Suppose you need a six-month loan before the subsidy arrives:
- Loan amount: 5 million yen
- Monthly interest rate: 0.75%
- Total interest: 5 million × 0.75% × 6 = 225,000 yen

This cost must be deducted from the subsidy proceeds.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Q1: If my company is short on cash, what should I do? Can the subsidy project be postponed?

**A:** Subsidies have strict execution deadlines and usually cannot be postponed. But you have three options:

First, **apply for financing immediately.** Prepare complete documents (the adoption notice, the project plan, the company's financial statements) and apply to the Japan Finance Corporation or Shoko Chukin Bank for "subsidy reservation financing"; such financing is approved quickly (five to seven days) and carries low interest rates (0.7–1.2%). Second, **negotiate deferred payment with suppliers.** After seeing the government subsidy notice, most Japanese suppliers will agree to a 60–90 day deferral. Tip: formally send a screenshot of the subsidy notice by Japanese-language email to show respect. Third, **apply for phased procurement.** Split a one-time purchase into three or four phases to reduce the pressure of each front-loading. If none of these work, the last resort is to **give up the subsidy program** and reapply once your cash is sufficient. Although this means missing out on the subsidy, it avoids the risk of a broken cash chain—in Japan, a broken cash chain leads to corporate bankruptcy.

### Q2: Will there be tax issues when the subsidy is paid? What do I need to prepare in advance?

**A:** This is an issue Chinese business owners easily overlook. Subsidies in Japan are **tax-exempt income** (not counted as corporate income under the Corporation Tax Act), but there are three important preconditions:

First, **the subsidy must be used for the project implementation.** If you obtained a 5-million-yen subsidy to buy equipment but later used it for another purpose (such as paying employee bonuses), that portion will be deemed taxable income by the tax office. Second, **complete accounting records are needed.** All subsidy-related income and expenditure must be clearly recorded in the books and handled separately under the "subsidy income" account. Third, **special notes are required when filing taxes.** In the annual income tax filing (決算書), there must be a "breakdown of the subsidy" (補助金の内訳). Recommended practice: when you apply for the subsidy, consult your tax accountant (会計士) at the same time so they handle it correctly at filing. After the subsidy arrives, do not transfer it directly into your general account; instead, first deposit it into a "dedicated subsidy account" (補助金専用口座) and then transfer it item by item as project expenses are incurred. That way, even in a tax audit, you have a complete chain of evidence.

### Q3: My subsidy application was rejected. Can I still recover the costs I fronted earlier?

**A:** It depends on the reason for and timing of the rejection. There are two cases:

**Case 1: Rejection before adoption** (the most common, such as a change in decision during the review period). In this case, **the company cannot obtain the subsidy, and the fronted costs cannot be recovered.** The only remedy is to convert these costs into the company's own investment expenditure, recording them as that year's fixed assets or R&D expenses for tax deduction. This is exactly why you should only front large sums after adoption.

**Case 2: Rejection during execution due to company reasons** (such as failing to procure as prescribed, or reimbursing with fake invoices). In this case the government not only rejects the subsidy but may also require the company to return the portion already paid, and it may even face legal penalties. So be sure to follow the subsidy's "implementation guidelines" (実施要領) strictly.

Prevention: before adoption, keep the small preparatory costs already incurred (such as consulting fees and design fees) under 500,000 yen; make large purchases only after adoption. Many subsidy programs offer adopted companies "partial coverage of pre-adoption preparation expenses" (採択前の準備経費の一部負担)—small in amount but helpful.

### Q4: If, at the reimbursement stage, the tax office deems it an "improper acquisition of a subsidy" (不正当な補助金獲得), what happens to the company?

**A:** This is the most serious risk scenario, and Japan's tax authorities penalize it very strictly. Possible consequences include:

First, **full return of the subsidy.** The government will require the company to return the entire subsidy within 60 days, plus a late-payment charge (an annual rate of 6% plus the local administrative body's rate, usually totaling 8–10%). Second, **additional tax** (加算税). Although the subsidy is tax-exempt, if it is deemed improperly acquired, the tax office may impose additional charges on other income related to the subsidy, with penalties as high as 40–50% of the actual tax amount. Third, **damage to the company's credit.** Being placed on the "list of companies that improperly acquired subsidies" (不正当な補助金獲得企業名簿) means that applications for other subsidies over the next three to five years will be rejected outright. Fourth, **criminal penalties** (in serious cases). If it involves false reimbursement, forged invoices, and the like, it may constitute the "crime of fraud" (詐欺罪), with a prison term of up to ten years.

Avoidance is simple: **strictly comply with all rules.** Specifically: (1) use genuine invoices (proper invoices from legitimate companies in Japan); (2) make all purchases through competitive bidding or at least three quotes; (3) keep complete procurement records, contracts, and delivery proofs; (4) undergo regular inspections by the subsidy execution body; (5) consult a professional subsidy advisor rather than relying on your own judgment. Chinese companies in particular, because of cultural differences and language issues, are more prone to errors in the details, so extra caution is warranted.

### Q5: After the subsidy arrives, do I still need to carry out follow-up reporting and inspections? For how long?

**A:** Yes. The subsidy cycle extends far beyond the moment the funds arrive. In fact, the work after the funds arrive is equally important and is easily overlooked by companies.

**The 4 phases after the subsidy arrives:**

**Phase 1: Completion inspection period (within 6 months of arrival).** The subsidy execution body will conduct "verification of the ex-post report" (事後報告書の検証) to confirm that the subsidy was really used for the project. They will ask you to submit the final purchase invoices, fixed-asset registration certificates, photos of use, and so on. A common mistake Chinese companies make at this stage is disorganized documentation—be sure to keep all original invoices, bank transfer records, contracts, and the like.

**Phase 2: On-site inspection period (6–18 months after arrival).** Many subsidy programs stipulate that, after the funds arrive, the government has the right to conduct on-site spot checks. You need to show the purchased equipment, the constructed project, and so on. Typically 10–20% of subsidy projects are spot-checked. During the inspection, government officials confirm whether the equipment actually exists and is used as prescribed. Recommendation: after procurement, photograph and archive all equipment, and attach labels with the subsidy project number.

**Phase 3: Financial reporting period (12–36 months after arrival).** Some subsidies require the company to conduct long-term financial tracking. For example, the "Management Innovation Subsidy" (経営革新補助金) requires the company to submit a "commercialization status report" (事業化状況報告) within three years of the subsidy's arrival, proving that the project indeed brought increased earnings to the company.

**Phase 4: Tax coordination period (within 5 years of arrival).** The tax office can conduct a tax audit within five years of the subsidy's arrival, re-confirming whether the tax treatment of the subsidy was correct. We recommend keeping all subsidy-related documents for at least five years.

**Time cost:** these inspections usually do not require the company to pay extra fees, but they consume the company's labor. We recommend setting aside 10–20 hours of inspection-cooperation time per subsidy project. If your company is small, you can hire a subsidy advisor or a Gyoseishoshi to assist, usually at a cost of 50,000–150,000 yen.

Japanese subsidies are real benefits that the government truly pays out. But the precondition is that you understand **how to handle the pressure of front-loading the subsidy**—which is precisely the core value of this article.
