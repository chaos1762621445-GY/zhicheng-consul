# What to Do When Your Subsidy Application Is Rejected: A Complete Guide to Reapplication and Appeals

More than 70% of Chinese business owners who have started companies in Japan have applied for some form of public funding (subsidies, grants, or incentive payments). Yet according to data from the Small and Medium Enterprise Agency, the average adoption rate is only 30%–50%, which means rejections are quite common. Many owners feel completely at a loss after receiving a "non-adoption" (rejection) notice—unsure whether to give up entirely or whether there is still a chance to try again.

This article walks you through your options after a subsidy rejection in detail, including analysis of the reasons for non-adoption, the reapplication process, the formal appeal procedure, and practical points to watch out for along the way.

## Understanding "Non-Adoption": Why Your Application Was Rejected

### The Main Reasons for Subsidy Rejections

The review process for Japanese subsidies is typically divided into two stages: **document screening** and **interview screening**. The reasons for rejection generally fall into the following categories.

**1. Common reasons for rejection at the document-screening stage**

According to official statistics from Japan's Small and Medium Enterprise Agency, roughly 60% of rejections occur at the document-screening stage. The most common reasons include:

- **Unclear logic in the business plan**: A weak connection between the subsidy's target and its content. For example, a manufacturing company applies for a "digital transformation subsidy," but the plan describes "how digitalization will boost competitiveness" only vaguely, making it hard for reviewers to grasp the path to implementation.

- **Errors in figures and financial data**: Arithmetic errors in the budget table, discrepancies with the company's actual financial statements, or investment amounts that are wildly out of line with market prices. For example, listing the purchase cost of a piece of machinery as JPY 1 million when its actual market price is only JPY 500,000.

- **Mismatch between business scale and the amount requested**: A micro-enterprise requesting an excessively large subsidy, or subsidy content that exceeds the company's current capabilities.

- **Incomplete or defective materials**: Missing required attachments, incomplete signatures and seals, or submission of an expired tax return.

**2. Reasons for rejection at the interview-screening stage**

Roughly 40% of rejections occur during the interview. The main reasons are:

- **Weak communication skills of the company representative**: Inability to clearly articulate the necessity and innovativeness of the subsidized project.
- **Disconnect between subsidy content and corporate strategy**: The interview reveals that the subsidized project lacks any link to the company's medium-term business plan.
- **Insufficient proof of self-funding**: Inability to adequately explain how the company will cover costs beyond the subsidy.
- **Weak risk awareness**: Insufficient consideration of problems that may arise during project implementation.

## Immediate Steps After a Subsidy Rejection: An Action Checklist

After receiving a non-adoption notice, do not rush to give up. Most Japanese subsidy programs allow for **reapplication** or a **formal appeal**, but you must act within the prescribed timeframe.

### Step 1: Gather Feedback (within 7 days of the rejection notice)

Once you have the non-adoption notice, you need to:

**1. Review the explanation of the reasons for rejection**

Most subsidy rejection notices come with a "reasons for non-adoption" statement. This typically points out:
- Specific problem areas identified during document screening
- Items that scored poorly
- Weaknesses relative to other applicant companies

**Example**: A restaurant business that applied for the "Business Succession and Transfer Subsidy" was rejected on the grounds that "the market analysis section of the business succession plan was inadequate." This shows the problem lay not in the financial statements but in the strategic argument.

**2. Request a detailed explanation of the reasons for rejection**

Many subsidy administration bodies offer a "reasons briefing" or "individual consultation session," and you can call or email to request a more detailed explanation of why you were rejected. For example:
- Japan Finance Corporation subsidy programs usually hold a two-week "review briefing" after a rejection notice
- Subsidy administration departments in each prefecture generally allow telephone inquiries

**3. Keep all related documents**

Make copies of the rejection notice and records of email correspondence; these documents will serve as references for a subsequent appeal or reapplication.

### Step 2: Weigh Your Three Options (within 2 weeks of the rejection notice)

After receiving a rejection notice, you face three paths:

| Path | When it applies | Time cost | Success rate |
|------|--------|---------|-------|
| **Reapply in the next round** | There is clear room for improvement and time to prepare thoroughly | 6–12 months | 35%–50% |
| **File an appeal** | You believe the rejection was procedurally flawed or the review was problematic | 1–2 months | 15%–25% |
| **Switch to another subsidy** | The current project is a poor fit and a better-matched subsidy exists | 2–6 months | 40%–60% |

## Reapplication Strategy: How to Improve Your Adoption Rate Next Time

If you decide to reapply in the next application round (usually 6–12 months later), this time you have valuable feedback in hand. According to tracking data from Japan's Small and Medium Enterprise Agency, an improved **reapplication has an adoption rate 35% higher than a first-time application**.

### Analyze the Reasons for Rejection and Draft an Improvement Plan

**Improvement strategies for document-screening rejections:**

**Scenario 1: The business plan is described unclearly**

*Original problem*: The "implementation content" section of the plan was too brief, describing a project involving both equipment investment and staff training in only 200 characters.

*Improvement plan*:
- Expand the relevant section to 800–1,200 characters
- Use a "timeline" structure: what happens in months 1–2, what happens in months 3–4
- Add a flowchart or Gantt chart so reviewers can see the project's progression steps at a glance
- Specify the responsible person and expected output for each phase

**Scenario 2: Financial data lacks precision**

*Original problem*: The budget for purchasing machinery was JPY 5 million, but no comparison of quotes from three or more vendors was provided.

*Improvement plan*:
- Collect at least 3 formal quotations (mitsumorisho) from different vendors
- Compare the differences among the three quotes in the plan and explain the reason for the final choice
- If purchasing imported equipment, note the exchange-rate calculation method and consideration of fluctuation risk
- Attach the equipment supplier's qualification certificates and technical specifications

**Scenario 3: Weak connection to corporate strategy**

*Original problem*: The company applied for a "digitalization subsidy," but the plan failed to clearly explain "why digitalize now."

*Improvement plan*:
- Add a "current-situation analysis" section at the beginning of the plan, letting the data speak:
  - The proportion of your business volume currently handled manually
  - The digitalization level of neighboring competitors
  - The frequency of customer complaints caused by manual processes
- In the "expected results" section, quantify the anticipated benefits with concrete figures:
  - Labor costs expected to fall 30% (from JPY 500,000 to JPY 350,000 per year)
  - Order response time shortened from 2 days to 4 hours
  - Customer satisfaction expected to rise above 90%

**Improvement strategies for interview-screening rejections:**

**Scenario 1: Lacking presentation and communication skills**

Interview rejections often stem from a lack of confidence or leaps in logic when explaining the subsidized project. Improvement plan:

- **Hold mock interviews 3 weeks in advance**: Invite a Japanese employee or a consultant to act as a reviewer and run at least 5 practice sessions
- **Prepare supplementary materials**: Create 5–10 slides using charts and photos to strengthen your presentation
- **Design standard answers for common questions**: List 20 likely questions and prepare concise answers (keep each under 1 minute)
- **Practice expressing numbers and amounts in Japanese**: Make sure you can smoothly deliver key figures at the interview, such as "年間売上高が1,500万円から2,000万円への拡大を見込んでいます" (we anticipate expanding annual sales from JPY 15 million to JPY 20 million)

**Scenario 2: A weak business foundation**

If the interviewer questions the company's financial situation, staffing, or management systems, the company's foundation needs reinforcing. Improvement plan:

- **Strengthen financial management**: Before reapplying, ensure that financial statements for the most recent 3 accounting years have all been filed with the tax authorities and that the books are clear
- **Reinforce staffing**: If the project involves a new field, recruit or train relevant personnel in advance and showcase their qualifications and experience in the application materials
- **Establish a risk-management mechanism**: Draw up contingency plans for problems during project implementation and present them in the supplementary materials

### Key Timing Milestones for Reapplication

Most Japanese government subsidies operate on an **annual cycle**:

- **Autumn of the prior year**: Administration bodies release the following year's subsidy solicitation announcement and application guidelines
- **Spring of the current year (April–May)**: The first-round solicitation usually closes
- **Autumn of the current year (August–September)**: The second-round solicitation closes

**Recommended timeline**:
1. June of the current year: Conduct detailed improvement planning (based on rejection feedback)
2. September–December of the current year: Prepare the reapplication materials for the following year
3. February of the following year: Submit the reapplication
4. May–June of the following year: Interview screening and announcement of results

## The Appeal Procedure: When You Believe the Rejection Was Wrong

If you believe the rejection itself involved a procedural error or an unfair review, you may file an appeal (異議申し立て). Under Japan's Administrative Procedure Act, a subsidy rejection is an administrative disposition, and the applicant has the right to file an appeal.

### Conditions for a Successful Appeal

An appeal is **not a retrial to overturn the outcome**, but a procedure with limited grounds. The chance of success is relatively low (about 15%–25%), but it is worth attempting in the following situations:

**1. Clear flaws in the review procedure**
- Important materials you submitted were overlooked or not reviewed by the evaluation committee
- A conflict of interest existed among the reviewers (e.g., a reviewer from a competing company)
- The reasons in the rejection notice contradict the score sheet

**2. The reasons for rejection are plainly self-contradictory**
- The notice says "the business plan is unclear," but that item was not scored low on the attached score sheet
- The reasons cite missing application materials, but you can prove the materials were submitted

**3. The subsidy administration body violated its own published review standards**
- The application guidelines clearly stipulate "requirement X," but the review did not score according to that standard

### The Specific Appeal Process

**Step 1: Confirm the appeal deadline (within 10–30 days of the rejection notice)**

Different subsidy programs set different appeal deadlines, usually:
- Subsidies directly administered by the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry: within 15 days of the rejection notice
- Prefectural subsidies: usually within 20–30 days

**Be sure to check the deadline on the rejection notice; once it passes, you can no longer appeal.**

**Step 2: Prepare the appeal document**

An appeal document must include the following:

```
[Template Structure]

Subsidy recipient (company name): □□□ Co., Ltd.
Representative Director: Taro Yamada
Date of filing: X月 X日, 2024

Title: Appeal against the non-adoption decision for the "□□ Subsidy"

[Part One] Grounds for the appeal
- Date the rejection decision notice was received
- Name of the subject subsidy and the application period
- Specific grounds for the appeal (choose from below)
  □ There was a procedural violation
  □ Submitted documents were not reviewed
  □ The review standards were clearly not followed

[Part Two] Specific supporting evidence
- Provide concrete evidence and reference documents
- Cite the relevant provisions in the application guidelines

[Part Three] Requested disposition
- Cancellation of the non-adoption decision
- Conduct of a re-review

Signature and seal
```

**Step 3: Submit the appeal document**

- Usually submitted to the **subsidy administration body** (not the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry itself, but its local branch or administrative service center)
- We recommend using **recorded-delivery mail (tokutei kiroku yubin) or courier** and keeping proof of delivery
- At the same time, keep an email version and send it to the official address as a backup

**Step 4: Await the appeal review result**

An appeal review usually takes 2–3 months. The administration body will:
- Notify you of the review's progress
- Request additional explanation if necessary
- Notify you of the review result in writing at the end

**Possible outcomes**:
- "Appeal granted" (異議申し立てを認める): the rejection is canceled, and a re-review or approval of the application is ordered
- "Appeal dismissed" (異議申し立てを棄却する): the appeal is denied and the original decision stands

### Realistic Expectations for an Appeal

According to public data from Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, the acceptance rate for subsidy appeals is only about 10%–20%. This is because:
- Most appeals are based on a misunderstanding of the scoring standards rather than a procedural error
- Evaluation committees have broad discretion, which the law restricts only lightly
- Only in cases of a **clear procedural violation** does an appeal have a chance of succeeding

**Therefore, unless you have genuinely found a procedural flaw, redirecting your energy toward reapplication will be more effective.**

## Switching to Another Subsidy: A New Path Opens

If the current subsidy is a poor fit, it is worth exploring other options. The Japanese government offers more than **400 types** of subsidies, of which 20–50 are typically suitable for any given company.

### Identifying a Better-Matched Subsidy

Your current rejection may be due to choosing the wrong type of subsidy. Here are some common mistakes:

**Scenario 1: A scale mismatch**

A solo entrepreneur applied for a "management-innovation subsidy for mid-sized companies," which is aimed mainly at SMEs with 50 or more employees. The application was rejected on the grounds that "the company scale does not match."

*Solution*: They should have applied for a "startup and venture subsidy" or a "grant for small-scale businesses."

**Scenario 2: Industry restrictions**

An agricultural company applied for an "advanced-technology adoption subsidy" and was rejected because that subsidy is limited to "manufacturing and IT services."

*Solution*: An agricultural company should apply for the "Agricultural Management Foundation Strengthening Reserve System" or "Management Income Stabilization Measures."

**Scenario 3: Regional restrictions**

A company in Shizuoka Prefecture applied for a subsidy from the "Tokyo Metropolitan Small and Medium Enterprise Promotion Public Corporation" and was rejected.

*Solution*: They should have applied for the local "Shizuoka Prefecture Subsidy for Strengthening SME Management Capabilities."

## Building Financial and Mental Resilience After a Failed Subsidy Application

### Financial Considerations

After a failed subsidy application, a company needs to assess **whether its own funds can complete the project independently**. This involves cash-flow planning:

**Case study**:
A food-trading company applied for the "Small-Scale Business Sustainability Subsidy" of JPY 1.5 million to purchase cold-chain logistics equipment. After the application was rejected, the company had two options:

1. **Abandon the project entirely**: Keep using the existing equipment, with market competitiveness likely declining year by year
2. **Self-fund the project**: Purchase the equipment using company reserves or a bank loan

From a financial standpoint, the company should calculate:
- The payback period of the equipment investment (usually 3–5 years)
- The opportunity cost of delaying the purchase (competitors may move first)
- The financing cost (bank loans usually carry interest of 3%–5%)

**A subsidy falling through does not mean the project must be abandoned. Often, self-funding the project is the wise move.**

### Psychological Adjustment and Long-Term Planning

From application to rejection, improvement, and reapplication, the entire cycle can last 12–18 months. Business owners can easily feel discouraged during this process. Our recommendations:

**1. Recognize that rejection is normal**
Japan's subsidy rejection rate is typically 40%–70%—the normal workings of a screening mechanism. The rejection rate for domestic internet-startup fundraising by investors is often even higher.

**2. Draft a medium-term subsidy plan**
Do not "bet it all" on a single subsidy. We recommend drawing up a 2-year subsidy application plan:

```
[Template]
- April 2024: Apply for ① Sustainability Subsidy (low risk, high success rate)
- August 2024: Apply for ② IT Adoption Subsidy (complementing ①)
- January 2025: Apply for ③ Business Succession Subsidy (preparing for expansion 2 years out)
```

The benefit of this approach is that it spreads the risk—even if ① is rejected, ② and ③ still have a chance of being adopted.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Q1: After a subsidy rejection, can I reapply for a second round in the same year?

**A:** Yes. Most subsidy programs allow multiple application rounds within the same fiscal year. For example, some subsidies have four solicitation rounds a year, and if the first round (closing in April) rejects you, you can enter the second round (closing in July). But note:

- Each round requires **entirely new application materials**; you cannot simply copy and paste
- You should make substantive improvements based on the first round's rejection feedback, or the second round may reject you as well
- Some subsidies stipulate that "the same business content may not be submitted multiple times within the same fiscal year," so be sure to confirm the guideline provisions before submitting
- If the second round rejects you again, it is better to switch to a reapplication in the following fiscal year and use the intervening months to prepare more thoroughly

A Fujianese business owner running a foreign-trade business applied for the "Sustainability Subsidy" in April 2024 and was rejected (the reason being that the market analysis was insufficient). When he reapplied in the second round in July, he doubled the market-analysis section of his plan, adding detailed competitor analysis and customer-research data, and was ultimately adopted in the second round.

### Q2: After my appeal is rejected, can I still reapply for the same subsidy?

**A:** Yes. The failure of an appeal will not affect your ability to apply for the same subsidy in subsequent years. However, we recommend:

- Thoroughly analyze **why the appeal failed** before reapplying. Usually it is because the rejection decision itself had no procedural flaw and your score was simply relatively low
- When reapplying, adopt the improvement suggestions from the original rejection notice rather than dwelling on procedural issues
- Wait at least **one application cycle** (usually 6–12 months) before reapplying so you have ample time to improve
- Keep all appeal materials and response documents; they can help you understand what the reviewers focus on

Legally, an administrative body has no authority to "retaliate" by rejecting a subsequent application simply because the applicant filed an appeal. In reality, however, some reviewers may form an unfavorable impression of a "difficult" applicant, so caution is advisable.

### Q3: Can I submit applications to several different subsidy administration bodies at the same time?

**A:** Yes, and it is a **smart move**. Japanese law permits companies to apply for multiple subsidies simultaneously, as long as the following conditions are met:

- **The no-double-subsidy principle**: The same business activity cannot receive duplicate funding from two different subsidies. For example, in purchasing a piece of machinery, you cannot obtain a subsidy for the same equipment from both the "Monozukuri Subsidy" and the "IT Adoption Subsidy"
- **Independence among subsidies**: Different subsidies may target different business content, objectives, or implementation periods

**In practice**:
An electronic-components manufacturer could apply simultaneously in 2024 for:
- Subsidy ①: "Monozukuri Subsidy" (to purchase production equipment; JPY 10M subsidy)
- Subsidy ②: "Human Resource Retention Support Grant" (for staff training; JPY 2M subsidy)
- Subsidy ③: "Regional Economy Revitalization Support Organization Dispatch Program" (for consulting fees; JPY 1M subsidy)

The three subsidies support different business content, so there is no double subsidy.

**Risk note**: When applying for multiple subsidies, you must **accurately disclose** on each application that you are applying for other subsidies simultaneously. Concealing this information constitutes dishonest application, and once discovered, not only will you be rejected but you may be placed on a "subsidy fraud blacklist," affecting your eligibility to apply for subsidies for the next 5–10 years.

### Q4: The rejection notice contains no detailed explanation. What should I do?

**A:** Some subsidy administration bodies write only "non-adoption" on the rejection notice without giving specific reasons. In that case, you have several ways to obtain information:

**Method 1: Telephone inquiry (usually effective within 2 weeks of rejection)**
- Call the subsidy administration body's inquiry line (usually printed on the notice)
- Have your application number (受付番号) and basic company information ready
- Politely say: "申し訳ございませんが、不採択理由についてご説明いただけますでしょうか？" (I am sorry to trouble you, but could you explain the reasons for non-adoption?)
- Usually the staff will point you in a general direction, such as "事業計画の実現可能性が低いと判断されました" (the feasibility of the business plan was judged to be low)

**Method 2: Attend the administration body's "feedback briefing" (フィードバック説明会)**
- Many subsidies hold briefings after rejections and invite applicants to attend
- At the briefing, the evaluation committee usually gives a group explanation, and you can also submit written questions

**Method 3: Engage a professional consultant**
- Subsidy consultants (organizations like ours) have contacts with the administration bodies and can sometimes obtain more detailed feedback than an ordinary applicant
- The fee is usually JPY 30,000–80,000, but it can save you valuable improvement time

**Method 4: Information disclosure request (情報公開請求)**
- Under Japan's Freedom of Information Act, you can request that a government body disclose the public information related to your application
- However, this route is quite formal, usually takes 1–3 months, and the information obtained is often limited (individual scoring opinions from reviewers are generally treated as personal privacy and not disclosed)

### Q5: Does a subsidy rejection negatively affect my company's credit score or loans?

**A:** A subsidy rejection **in itself does not** affect a company's credit score or bank loans. This is because:

- A subsidy rejection is a **policy judgment**, not an assessment of business management ability. Banks and credit agencies only look at hard data such as the company's financial statements and debt-repayment record
- Subsidy application information is generally not reported to financial institutions or credit-reporting agencies
- A bank's assessment of a company is based mainly on: sales, profit, cash flow, debt ratio, loan-repayment history, and so on

**But there is one exception**: If a company **falsifies financial data or materials** when applying for a subsidy, then once discovered, not only will the subsidy application be rejected but it may also be reported to the relevant authorities, which can affect its credit.

**On the contrary, receiving a subsidy often helps with financing**:
- A bank will regard receiving a government subsidy as evidence that the feasibility of the company's project has been verified by a third party
- The detailed business plan prepared when applying for a subsidy can also serve as strong material when seeking bank financing

If the rejected subsidy project is itself important to the company, the company may consider **borrowing from a bank to complete the project** rather than letting the subsidy rejection stand in its way.
