# How to Write a Subsidy Business Plan? Writing Points That Double Your Adoption Rate

When applying for various subsidies in Japan (grants, subsidies), the quality of your business plan directly determines your adoption rate. Over the past five years I have served more than 200 Chinese-owned businesses in Japan, and I have found that many applicants are rejected because their plans are not professional enough—some are even judged as having "low feasibility" and eliminated.

This article will teach you, from a hands-on perspective, how to write a subsidy business plan that dramatically boosts your adoption rate.

## 1. Understand the Central Role of the Business Plan in Subsidy Review

### Why is the business plan so important?

Subsidy reviewers usually skim your plan in 5–10 minutes. Within that limited time, they need to judge:

1. **Whether your business aligns with the subsidy's policy objectives**
2. **Whether you have the capability to execute this project**
3. **Whether the return on investment is reasonable**
4. **Whether risk management is adequate**

According to statistics from Japan's Small and Medium Enterprise Agency, the average adoption rate for general subsidies in FY2023 was 35–45%, but a well-structured plan can raise that rate to 60–70%. In other words, a well-written plan increases your odds of success by a full 50–100%.

### The weight of the business plan

Within the review framework, the business plan typically accounts for:
- **Technical review**: 40–50%
- **Policy alignment**: 25–35%
- **Financial feasibility**: 15–25%

This means that no matter how strong your technology is, if your plan is vague and unclear, the review committee may be unable to fully grasp your value proposition.

## 2. The Standard Structure and Content Framework of a Subsidy Business Plan

### Part One: Business Overview (Executive Summary)

This is the most critical part; it determines whether the reviewers are willing to read on. Keep the length to **300–500 characters**.

**Essential elements to cover:**

1. **Current business situation and problems** (150 characters)
   - Concrete figures: your revenue, market share, number of employees
   - For example: "We are a Chinese-owned company selling Japanese cookware. Our revenue last year was 8.5 million JPY, and our core problem is that overseas procurement costs have risen 30%, driving our profit margin down from 12% to 8%."

2. **Use of the subsidy and improvement effects** (150 characters)
   - Concrete amounts: how much you are applying for and where it will be spent
   - Expected outcomes, expressed in numbers, such as "By introducing new equipment, we plan to raise production efficiency by 25% and grow annual revenue to 12 million JPY"

3. **A brief explanation of feasibility** (100–150 characters)
   - What advantages do you have to realize this plan? Capital, technology, talent, experience

### Part Two: Analysis of Management Issues

Many applicants make mistakes here—either they state the issues too broadly, or they fail to clearly explain the connection to the subsidy.

**The correct approach:**

**Use the SWOT analysis framework**

| Factor | Specific content |
|------|--------|
| **Strengths (S)** | We are the most trusted supplier in Japan's Chinese community, with 500+ stable customers accumulated |
| **Weaknesses (W)** | Aging warehousing facilities, with inventory turnover below the industry average |
| **Opportunities (O)** | Japan's aging society; the health-food market is growing 10% annually |
| **Threats (T)** | Intensifying competition from large online platforms is squeezing profit margins |

Starting from this analysis, your issue becomes **concrete and measurable**:

> "Our core issue is the low efficiency of our warehousing facilities (weakness), which prevents us from meeting rapidly growing market demand in a timely manner (an opportunity not fully exploited). Through facility modernization funded by this subsidy, we can shorten inventory turnover from the current 45 days to 30 days, thereby capturing the dividends of market growth."

### Part Three: Business Plan Content

This is the part the reviewers care about most, and it needs to be **detailed but not verbose**.

**1. Business strategy**
- Market analysis: the size, growth rate, and competitive landscape of your target market
- Concrete example: "The Japanese Chinese-food market is worth roughly 60 billion JPY and is growing 8% per year. We aim to raise our market share from 1.2% to 2.5% within three years, corresponding to revenue growth to 15 million JPY."

**2. Implementation plan**
Present it with a Gantt chart or timeline:

```
Months 1–2: Equipment procurement and installation
Months 3–6: Staff training and system tuning
Months 7–12: Full-capacity operation and effect verification
Months 13–24: Expanded application and continuous improvement
```

**3. Structure and personnel**
Clearly list:
- Who is responsible for project management
- The experience and professional qualifications of the implementation team
- If necessary, explain external consultants or partner suppliers

**Example:** "The project is fully led by me personally; I have 15 years of experience in Japanese retail. Manager Li, head of the production department, once led a production-optimization project at a large manufacturing enterprise that cut costs by 20%."

## 3. Key Points for Writing the Financial Plan

The review committee places extreme importance on the **reasonableness and conservatism** of financial assumptions.

### The correct approach to revenue forecasting

**Don't do this:**
> "After using the subsidy, our revenue will grow significantly"

**Do this instead:**

Make conservative estimates based on historical data and market research:

```
Average revenue growth rate over the past 3 years: 6%
Market growth rate: 8%
Effect lag period in Year 1 after implementation (reserve 3 months)

Forecast model:
Base revenue (2024): 9 million JPY
Year 1 growth rate: 4% (conservative estimate, below the market growth rate)
Year 1 forecast revenue: 9.36 million JPY
Year 2 growth rate: 8%
Year 2 forecast revenue: 10.11 million JPY
Year 3 growth rate: 10%
Year 3 forecast revenue: 11.12 million JPY
```

This kind of forecast earns trust more easily.

### The granularity of the expenditure plan

The most common rejection reason in subsidy applications is "unclear expenditure details."

**You must itemize in detail:**

| Item | Amount | Basis |
|------|------|------|
| Manufacturing equipment A | 3,500,000 JPY | Average of quotes from 3 suppliers |
| Installation and tuning | 450,000 JPY | Supplier contract quote |
| Operation training | 200,000 JPY | 5 people × 8 hours × 5,000 JPY/hour |
| Shipping and taxes | 350,000 JPY | Estimated at 10% of equipment cost |
| **Total** | **4,500,000 JPY** | |

**Subsidy application amount**: 3,000,000 JPY (75%)
**Own funds**: 1,500,000 JPY (25%)

Note: Most subsidies require the applicant to bear 20–30% of the cost, which demonstrates your commitment to the project.

### Payback Period and ROI Calculation

A question the reviewers will inevitably ask: "When will you break even?"

**Sample calculation:**

```
Subsidy investment amount: 3,000,000 JPY
Expected annual net-profit increase (through efficiency gains): 600,000 JPY
Payback period: 3,000,000 ÷ 600,000 = 5 years

But if you also explain:
- Years 1–2 annual increase: 300,000 JPY
- Years 3–5 annual increase: 600,000 JPY
- Cumulative 5-year net gain: 2,400,000 JPY

This demonstrates the project's long-term value even if it hasn't fully broken even.
```

## 4. Five Writing Techniques That Double the Adoption Rate

### Technique 1: Use the "Problem–Solution–Effect" syllogism

Present your logic in a structured way:

```
[Problem]: The current warehousing facilities are outdated; picking work takes 120 minutes/day
           Loss: annual salaries of 3 employees × 20% efficiency loss ≈ 2 million JPY

[Solution]: Introduce an automated picking system (investment: 3 million JPY)
            New system picking time: 60 minutes/day

[Quantified effect]: Annual labor-cost savings of 2 million JPY
                     Equipment investment payback period: 1.5 years
                     Cumulative 3-year net gain: 4.5 million JPY
```

This structure lets reviewers grasp your logic at a glance.

### Technique 2: Introduce third-party data and evidence

Don't rely entirely on your own claims.

**A persuasive approach:**
- Cite industry reports: "According to the Small and Medium Enterprise Agency's 2024 white paper, automation investment in manufacturing raises production efficiency by an average of 28%"
- Show supplier contracts and quotations
- Provide customer testimonials or market-research data

**Example:** "According to a 2023 survey by the Japan Food Distribution News, the health-food category grew 12% annually, higher than the overall food industry's 6%. Our target market has ample growth potential."

### Technique 3: Risk identification and countermeasures

Many applicants avoid mentioning risks; in fact, the opposite is true—**a plan that recognizes risks and proposes countermeasures earns more trust**.

**Example:**

| Risk item | Likelihood | Impact | Countermeasure |
|---------|---------|--------|--------|
| Supplier delivery delays | Medium | High | Sign contracts with 3 suppliers to ensure backups |
| Overly long staff-training cycle | Low | Medium | Hire a professional training firm and arrange in advance |
| Market demand below expectations | Low | Medium | Build a flexible production plan with adjustable capacity |

### Technique 4: Replace abstract descriptions with concrete cases

**A poor statement:**
> "This project will greatly enhance the company's competitiveness"

**A good statement:**
> "Referring to a peer company's implementation case (Company A carried out a similar project in 2022), production costs fell 15% and the market response rate improved by 8 percentage points. Based on a conservative estimate, we expect to achieve a 12% cost reduction."

### Technique 5: Visualize complex information

Subsidy plans usually must not exceed 10–15 pages. The most effective approach is to combine charts:

- **Flowcharts**: show the timeline of project implementation
- **Line charts**: show the projected changes in revenue, profit, and cost
- **Comparison charts**: contrast the current state vs. the post-improvement effect
- **Organization charts**: show the project's responsible parties and authority structure

## 5. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Q1: How long should my plan be? Is there a word limit?

**A:** Most Japanese subsidy plans have a clear page requirement, usually 10–15 A4 pages. More detail is not necessarily better. The key is to maximize information density within the limited space. A recommended structure is: business overview (2 pages) → issue analysis (2 pages) → business plan (4 pages) → financial plan (3 pages) → risk management (1–2 pages) → appendices (quotations, supporting documents, etc.). We advise clients to first complete a detailed version (20–30 pages), then trim it down to the officially required version. This ensures logical completeness without exceeding the page limit. Many Chinese business owners write over 30 pages in an effort to "explain fully," which instead causes reviewer fatigue and hurts their scores.

### Q2: Do the data forecasts in a subsidy plan need to be 100% accurate?

**A:** Not at all. Reviewers understand that forecasts inherently involve uncertainty. They focus on three aspects: (1) Is the forecasting logic reasonable—did you base it on historical data and market research, or on wishful thinking? (2) Are the forecast assumptions conservative—it is generally recommended that your projected growth rate be below 80–90% of the industry average; (3) Is the causal relationship between the forecast and the project clear—why will investing in this project produce this result? I have seen many applications rejected because the forecasts were overly optimistic (projecting 30% annual growth when the industry average is only 8%), or because the forecast was unrelated to the project (claiming that investing in warehousing equipment would increase overseas sales, without explaining the logic). The safest approach is to attach a professional market-research report or industry statistics as support.

### Q3: If we are a startup with no historical revenue data, how should we write it?

**A:** Startups need to use the "analogy method" rather than the "historical method" for forecasting. Specifically: (1) Find a mature company with a similar business model and analyze its early growth curve; (2) Reference industry benchmarks—your target market's average growth rate, the market share of new entrants, and so on; (3) Conduct market research—demand data derived from questionnaires, focus groups, or interviews; (4) Forecast conservatively—a new company's growth typically lags established firms by 6–12 months. For example, when a Chinese-owned food startup applied for a subsidy, it could not provide its own revenue data, but by researching competitors and purchasing industry reports, it described in detail its market entry point, target customers, sales channels, and expected sales volume, ultimately winning adoption. Startups can actually gain bonus points for "strong innovation"; the key is proving that your assumptions are based on solid market research.

### Q4: How should we describe our company's strengths and competitive advantages in the plan?

**A:** Avoid generic praise. Don't write vague statements like "our corporate culture is excellent" or "our team is highly capable." Instead, speak with "verifiable facts." For example: "We have a 15-year brand history in Japan's Chinese community, with 500+ stable B2B customers and a 98% customer retention rate" (this is verifiable); "Core management team members average 12 years of industry experience, and 4 of them once held management positions at large Japanese manufacturers" (provide résumés as proof); "We applied for and successfully executed Japan's IT Adoption Subsidy, accumulating government-project management experience" (a certificate copy can be provided). What reviewers value most is "execution capability"—your track record of completing similar projects. If you have none, be honest about it, but at the same time demonstrate the team's learning ability, external consulting support, and other compensating measures.

### Q5: Can the plan be revised after submission? If rejected, can I apply again?

**A:** This depends on the specific subsidy's rules. Most one-time subsidies (general subsidies, small-business subsidies) have clear application periods and deadlines, and revisions are generally not accepted after the deadline. However, within the application period, many subsidy bodies allow a certain number of resubmissions. We recommend conducting multiple rounds of internal review before formal submission, or seeking revision advice from a professional consultant. Regarding reapplication: if you are rejected this fiscal year, most subsidies reopen for applications the following year. In our experience, the adoption rate of a revised reapplication can rise 30–50%, because you have already received the reviewers' feedback (some bodies provide a brief summary of rejection reasons). But note: don't simply resubmit the rejected plan as-is—you should make targeted improvements based on the rejection reasons (such as "unclear issue definition," "untrustworthy financial forecast," or "low feasibility"). For first-time applicants, I advise Chinese business owners to reserve ample time to polish the plan with a consultant, rather than rushing the submission.

## 6. Practical Checklist: A Pre-Submission Checklist

Before submitting your subsidy plan, check each item:

- [ ] Can the business overview make a stranger understand my plan within 3 minutes?
- [ ] Is the issue defined specifically enough, quantifying the current state and goals with numbers?
- [ ] Does the proposed solution have a direct causal relationship with the issue?
- [ ] Is the financial forecast based on verifiable assumptions and data sources?
- [ ] Have I listed at least 3 potential risks and countermeasures?
- [ ] Have I used charts to visualize the key information?
- [ ] Have I stayed within the officially required page or word limit?
- [ ] Does all data and every citation include a source note?
- [ ] Is the plan's execution timeline and steps realistic and feasible?
- [ ] Are the team members' experience and qualifications backed by documentation?
