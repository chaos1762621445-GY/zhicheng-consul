# The 5 Most Common Reasons Japanese Subsidy Applications Fail (And How to Avoid Them)

Chinese business owners in Japan often feel both hopeful and confused about Japan's subsidy system. According to Japan's Small and Medium Enterprise Agency, roughly 35% of subsidy applications are rejected each year, and a large share of those applicants fall into the very same "traps." As a subsidy consultant serving hundreds of Chinese-owned businesses in Japan, I have found that these failures are entirely avoidable. This article breaks down the five core reasons Japanese subsidy applications fail and offers practical methods to steer clear of them.

## Failure Reason One: Careless Document Review — Errors and Omissions in Basic Information

### Why is this a "fatal wound"?

It sounds basic, but the statistics show that **more than 40% of rejected subsidy cases stem from document-quality problems.** Japanese subsidy screening applies a "zero-tolerance" principle — even a single figure in the application that doesn't match the financial statements, or an inconsistency in how the company name is written in kanji, can send the entire application back for re-review.

I once served a Chinese-owned food import company whose subsidy application was rejected for a startling reason: the year of establishment written in the business plan didn't match the registry (one said April 2018, the other March 2018). That three-month discrepancy ultimately wasted a JPY 50,000 application fee and three months of time.

### Concrete Methods to Avoid It

**Step 1: Establish a "triple-check mechanism"**

- **First check**: Self-review by the applicant. Verify every figure line by line against the company's financial statements, registry, and bank accounts. Prepare a "verification checklist" covering 15 key items, including: company name, date of establishment, registered capital, number of employees, revenue over the past three years, tax ID, and so on.

- **Second check**: Review by a finance staffer or accountant. If you have no dedicated finance staff, at the very least have the accountant who does your books go through all the financial data. They will catch statement inconsistencies you might overlook.

- **Third check**: Third-party review. Ideally, commission a subsidy consultant or a Japanese law firm to look for problems with a professional eye. The cost is usually JPY 30,000–50,000, but it can effectively prevent losses more than ten times that amount.

**Step 2: Manage application materials in a standardized way**

We recommend every applicant prepare a "document index" listing the latest version, revision date, and source department for each file. Many companies, in a rush, end up submitting outdated or incorrect versions.

**Step 3: Pay special attention to these three "high-risk zones"**

1. **Date consistency**: The dates on your business license, financial statements, and application form must match exactly.
2. **Amount matching**: The requested amount must align with the investment budget in the business plan.
3. **Personnel information**: The seals and signatures of the company's legal representative, application lead, and finance lead must all be present and consistent.

## Failure Reason Two: An Unconvincing Business Plan with No Data to Back It Up

### This is the review committee's "top priority"

The core logic of Japanese subsidy screening is: **Can your business plan create real value for the regional economy or industrial development?** The quality of the business plan (事業計画書) directly determines whether your subsidy is approved.

In my experience, **more than 50% of failed applications actually fail at this stage** — the plan is either too vague, lacks data support, or sets unrealistic targets.

Typical "point-losing" issues include:

- Vague plan descriptions (broad goals such as "boost sales" or "improve management")
- No data at all: no market research data, no competitive analysis, not even financial projections
- Targets disconnected from reality (e.g., a small company with JPY 5 million in annual revenue proposing to quintuple that revenue within one year)

### Concrete Methods to Avoid It

**Step 1: Build your business plan on the "SMART goals framework"**

- **S (Specific)**: Be concrete. Don't say "expand sales"; say "by opening a flagship store, increase Japan-market sales from JPY 5 million to JPY 12 million per year."
- **M (Measurable)**: Set clear numerical targets: invest JPY 1 million, expect a 30% revenue increase, create 5 jobs.
- **A (Achievable)**: Base it on historical data and market research, not on wishful thinking.
- **R (Relevant)**: Align it with the industrial policy and regional development the subsidy requires.
- **T (Time-bound)**: Specify achievement within 3 years, within 5 years.

A success story: a Chinese-owned creative handicrafts company applied for an SME subsidy in the Kinki region (subsidy rate 50%, cap JPY 2 million). Their plan spelled out in detail:

- Research data: a survey of 150 retailers and 100 consumers
- Competitive analysis: the characteristics and market share of 5 competing firms
- Financial projections: detailed 3-year revenue forecasts (Year 1 growth 15%, Year 2 growth 25%, Year 3 growth 20%)
- Job creation: a plan to hire 3 new full-time and 5 part-time employees

In the end they received a JPY 1.5 million subsidy, while another company applying in the same round was rejected because its plan was too simple (just one page).

**Step 2: Legitimize your data sources**

Every piece of data must have a source. We recommend including:

- Official data: figures from Japan's Statistics Bureau and prefectural statistics departments
- Market research reports: you can purchase reports from professional research institutions (such as industry analyses from Teikoku Databank)
- Independent research: if you conducted a survey, attach the questionnaire sample and methodology
- Past performance data: your own company's historical financial results

**Step 3: Present the key data tables**

Your business plan must include tables such as the following:

| Metric | Current | After 1 Year | After 2 Years | After 3 Years |
|------|-------|-------|-------|-------|
| Annual revenue | 50 million | 57.5 million | 66.12 million | 76.04 million |
| Profit margin | 12% | 15% | 18% | 20% |
| Employees | 15 | 18 | 22 | 25 |

Tables like this are far more persuasive than prose.

## Failure Reason Three: A Mismatch Between Your Business Type and the Subsidy Category — Picking the Wrong "Lane"

### Japanese subsidies "come in categories" — you can't just apply blindly

A common mistake among Chinese business owners in Japan is: they see a subsidy and apply for it directly, without any regard for whether it suits their type of business.

Japan's subsidy system is dizzyingly varied. Different subsidies target different:

- **Industry fields**: manufacturing, services, agriculture, tourism, IT, green energy, and more
- **Company sizes**: micro-enterprises (no more than 20 employees), SMEs (no more than 300), large enterprises
- **Purposes**: technological innovation, export promotion, regional revitalization, women's entrepreneurship, employment of people with disabilities, and more
- **Regions**: nationwide, specific prefectures, specific municipalities

**Pick the wrong subsidy and you will be rejected no matter how good everything else is.**

A real case: a Chinese-owned beauty salon applied for the "SME technological innovation subsidy" (ものづくり補助金) on the grounds that "we want to buy the latest beauty equipment — that's innovation." It was rejected, because that subsidy is explicitly aimed at "manufacturing or processing," and the beauty-services industry falls outside its scope.

### Concrete Methods to Avoid It

**Step 1: Draw up a "subsidy-matching matrix"**

Before applying, assess yourself against the following dimensions:

```
Does my company meet the requirements for:
□ Industry category? (manufacturing, services, trade, etc.)
□ Company size? (employee count, capital range)
□ Regional restrictions? (limited to certain prefectures or municipalities?)
□ Age requirements? (in operation for at least how many years?)
□ Nationality restrictions? (does it explicitly require a Japanese-national company — note: most subsidies require the legal representative to hold Japanese nationality or permanent residency, a common "hidden restriction")
```

**Step 2: Proactively consult SME support organizations**

- **Nationwide**: local Chambers of Commerce and Industry (商工会議所) and Societies of Commerce and Industry (商工会) offer free consultations
- **For Chinese business owners in Japan**: if there is a Chinese chamber of commerce or Chinese business federation in your area, they can also offer advice
- **Official resources**: visit the website of Japan's SME Agency (www.chusho.meti.go.jp), which has a "subsidy search system" (補助金検索)

Enter your company information into this system and it will automatically match and recommend which subsidies suit you.

**Step 3: Carefully check the "trap clauses"**

Many subsidies look like a perfect fit but hide "implicit conditions":

1. **Project-location restrictions**: the subsidy requires the business to be carried out in a specific area (e.g., the subsidy stipulates R&D must be conducted in the North Kanto region, but your R&D center is in Tokyo)
2. **Employment obligations**: some subsidies require you to increase headcount during the subsidy period and prohibit layoffs
3. **Reporting responsibilities**: after a successful application, you must submit quarterly progress reports over 3–5 years, and any deviation may trigger a demand for repayment
4. **Subsidy-rate caps**: many subsidies advertise a 50% subsidy rate but actually have a maximum amount (e.g., up to JPY 2 million)

## Failure Reason Four: An Unreasonable Cost-Benefit Budget the Subsidy Can't Cover

### Why does the review committee reject budgets that look "reasonable"?

Subsidy screening not only assesses whether your project is meaningful — it also rigorously evaluates **the reasonableness of your costs.** If your cost estimates are clearly above market prices, or your benefit projections are obviously inflated, the committee has grounds to doubt your project's feasibility.

Common problems include:

- **Procurement prices too high**: for the same equipment, Supplier A quotes JPY 5 million and Supplier B quotes JPY 3 million — why choose A? If you can't reasonably explain it, you'll be seen as "wasting the subsidy."
- **Inflated labor costs**: if you set your technical staff's monthly salary at JPY 800,000 when the industry average is only JPY 500,000, the committee will question it.
- **Mismatched benefit expectations**: investing in JPY 10 million of equipment and expecting to recover it in the very first year looks "unrealistic" in the reviewers' eyes.

### Concrete Methods to Avoid It

**Step 1: Run a "three-way bid"**

Before applying, obtain quotes from at least three suppliers for every large procurement item. State clearly in the business plan:

- Supplier A: XXX machine, priced at JPY 6 million
- Supplier B: same model, priced at JPY 5 million
- Supplier C: same model, priced at JPY 4.8 million
- **Reason for selection**: Supplier C offers the best price, plus a 5-year warranty and a technical-support commitment

The benefits of doing this:

1. It proves to the review committee that you are "cost-conscious"
2. Even if the finalized subsidy lowers the approved procurement amount, you have room to adjust
3. It avoids rejection for prices "clearly above market"

**Step 2: Validate costs with a "comparable benchmarking method"**

For every cost item, find a comparable reference:

- **Equipment procurement**: check the market prices of similar products on Japanese industrial-goods platforms (such as MonotaRO, Amazon Business)
- **Labor costs**: refer to the "effective jobs-to-applicants ratio" and "average wage" data for your area (published on the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare website)
- **Operating costs**: benchmark against peers in the same industry to ensure your estimates aren't wildly off

**Step 3: Carefully design the "payback period"**

The review committee values **sustainability.** If your project can recover its investment within a reasonable timeframe, it will greatly improve your approval odds.

Recommended practices:

- For general equipment investments, set a payback period of 3–5 years
- Provide a detailed cash-flow projection, breaking down returns year by year
- If the purpose is to expand sales, prove that the added revenue can cover the investment cost

Take a food-manufacturing company that applied for a subsidy to invest JPY 5 million in a new production line:

| Year | Capacity Increase | Added Revenue | Added Cost | Added Profit | Cumulative Payback |
|------|---------|-----------|---------|---------|---------|
| Year 1 | +30% | 20 million | 12 million | 8 million | 8 million |
| Year 2 | +30% | 24 million | 13 million | 11 million | 19 million |
| Year 3 | +20% | 28.8 million | 14 million | 14.8 million | 33.8 million |

By presenting it clearly in a table like this, the JPY 5 million investment is fully recovered within two and a half years, which greatly boosts the approval odds.

## Failure Reason Five: Flaws in How or When Materials Are Submitted — A Technical Rejection

### The Japanese government's strictness on "formal review" is beyond imagination

This is the most frustrating reason to fail — there's nothing wrong with your project and your plan is excellent, but you're rejected simply because of **a problem with how you applied.** Japan's administrative system demands procedural propriety to an almost "rigid" degree.

Common grounds for "technical rejection" include:

- Submitting past the deadline (even one minute late means it won't be accepted)
- Required seals or signatures missing or inconsistent with the application form
- Using an outdated version of the application form
- PDF file format non-compliant (some subsidies require the PDF to be generated in a specific way)
- Uploaded files in the wrong order or missing required attachments

I once saw a Chinese company whose subsidy application was excellent in every respect, but the whole application was rejected because the legal representative's seal was "not clear enough" (a photocopy-quality issue). It only passed after resubmission — wasting an extra month.

### Concrete Methods to Avoid It

**Step 1: Create an "application checklist"**

Before submitting, go through this checklist item by item:

```
□ Is the application form the latest version? (check the release date)
□ Have all required fields been filled in?
□ Are the seals of both the legal representative and the application lead affixed?
□ Are the seals clearly legible?
□ Are all signatures in black ink?
□ Is the date format consistent? (Japan often uses the Japanese era or the Western calendar — confirm the subsidy's requirement)
□ Are the attachments arranged in the specified order?
□ Is the number of copies of every document correct? (usually the original plus 3 copies)
□ Is the company seal also required? (many subsidies require both the company seal and the legal representative's seal)
□ Mail or online submission? If by mail, have you prepared a registered-mail envelope?
□ What is the submission deadline? Must it be received by that date, or does the postmark date count?
```

**Step 2: Adopt an "early-submission strategy"**

Even if the deadline is March 31, we recommend submitting by March 25. There are three reasons:

1. If you find a problem with your materials, you still have time to fix and resubmit them
2. It avoids postal delays and other uncontrollable factors
3. Online systems sometimes "jam" in the hours before the deadline; submitting early avoids upload failures

**Step 3: Build a "key-date reminder table"**

For most subsidies, the application window is usually only 1–2 months, with just one or two rounds per year.

We recommend that you:

1. Subscribe to email reminders from Japan's SME Agency (you can register on the website)
2. Mark the application window, deadline, and review period for every major subsidy on your calendar
3. Start preparing materials one month in advance

An example schedule for common subsidy applications:

| Subsidy Name | Application Window | Deadline | Review Period | Results Announced |
|-----------|--------|--------|--------|---------|
| ものづくり補助金 | mid-January | late February | March–May | June |
| 小規模事業者持続化補助金 | rolling | each round's deadline | 1.5 months | the following month |
| 事業再構築補助金 | mid-March | late April | May–June | July |

**Step 4: Commission a professional for a final review**

Before submitting, it's best to have a third party who wasn't involved in the project (such as a consultant, lawyer, or accountant) review the materials one more time. With "fresh eyes," they can catch issues you and your team may have missed.

This step costs a JPY 500–1,000 consultation fee but avoids the risk of failure — well worth it.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Q1: As a Chinese national on a Business Manager visa (with a non-Japanese legal representative), can I apply for Japanese subsidies?

**A:** This is what many Chinese residents in Japan care about most. The answer is: **yes, but with limits.** Most Japanese subsidies have no explicit "Japanese-nationality requirement" for the legal representative, but they generally require "a corporation or individual with a place of business in Japan" (日本国内に事業所を有する法人または個人). If you have completed corporate registration and hold a Japanese bank account and tax records, you can apply for most subsidies. However, some specific subsidies explicitly require the applicant to hold "Japanese nationality" or "permanent residency" — this usually appears in subsidies involving government procurement, security-sensitive industries, and the like. We recommend proactively consulting your local Chamber of Commerce and Industry before applying; they can give an authoritative judgment. Also, even if you meet the legal requirements, poorly prepared Japanese documents can hamper the review committee's understanding, so we recommend hiring a professional to help translate and organize your materials.

### Q2: My subsidy application was rejected last year — can I reapply for the same subsidy this year?

**A:** Yes, but note two things. First, you must **fully understand why you were rejected last time.** Japanese subsidy rejection notices (不採択通知) state the reasons, which usually include "the business plan has low feasibility" (事業計画の実現可能性が低い), "the cost-effectiveness is insufficient" (費用対効果が不十分), and so on. Your next application must make substantive improvements addressing these issues, not simply repackage the same content. Second, **many subsidies have a policy that "previously unsuccessful applicants may also apply,"** but you need to demonstrate that you have made improvements. Before reapplying, we recommend consulting the subsidy's administering body (usually at the Chamber of Commerce and Industry or an SME support center); they will sometimes offer "informal feedback" to help you understand the real reasons for rejection. Some consultant teams can even arrange a candid discussion with review members to clarify the direction for improvement.

### Q3: I've completed my subsidy application and passed review, and I'm now executing the project. But I've suddenly hit changed circumstances (a supplier price hike, a planned hire unable to start, etc.) — can I request a change to the subsidy amount?

**A:** This is a very practical question. Japan's subsidy management system allows applicants to make a **"business plan change" (事業計画変更)** during project execution, but strict conditions apply. First, the reason for the change must be "a genuinely unforeseeable factor," not "I underestimated the cost." Second, the change cannot violate the subsidy's original purpose — if the subsidy is for "expanding capacity for Product A," you can't switch it to "investing in Product B." Third, many changes require resubmitting application documents and obtaining written approval from the administering body, a process that can take 1–2 months. The key advice is: **the moment you notice a problem in execution, report it to the subsidy administering body immediately, rather than making the change on your own.** Many Chinese business owners think "I'll change it first and explain later," only to face fines or repayment demands when it's discovered. Subsidy administering bodies (such as the Chamber of Commerce and Industry) are far more lenient toward "changes that are proactively reported."

### Q4: If the subsidy isn't fully used up, can the leftover money be repurposed?

**A:** **Absolutely not** — that would be against the law. A core principle of Japanese subsidies is "the use of the subsidy is strictly limited" (補助金の使途は厳格に限定される). If the subsidy's designated use is "purchasing production equipment," you may only use it to purchase production equipment. Even seemingly related expenses (such as shipping or installation fees) cannot be paid if they fall outside the approved scope. If there is a genuine surplus at the end (say the approved procurement was JPY 2 million but you only spent JPY 1.8 million), that money must be **returned to the government in full** — it cannot be "used flexibly." Violating this principle can be deemed "false reporting" or "fraud," with very serious consequences: not only repayment, but potential criminal liability and an inability to apply for any government subsidy for the next 5 years. So our advice: before executing the project, confirm clearly with the administering body "what you can and cannot spend on," to avoid disputes after the fact.

### Q5: I've heard some companies successfully obtained subsidies with the help of "consulting firms." How do I choose a reliable consulting firm and avoid being scammed?

**A:** The subsidy consulting market does indeed vary widely in quality. Key criteria for choosing a consultant: First, **don't believe promises of a "guaranteed approval."** Any honest consultant will tell you they can raise your approval rate but cannot guarantee 100% approval. Second, **check the consultant's credentials.** Good subsidy consultants usually hold one of the following: registered SME Management Consultant (中小企業診断士), certified financial advisor, or a record of 3+ years of subsidy-application experience. You can look up a consultant's background on the Japan SME Management Consultant Association website (www.jsbri.or.jp). Third, **the fee model should be reasonable.** The recommended structure is "a fixed consulting fee plus a success fee after the subsidy is approved" (e.g., a consulting fee of JPY 50,000–100,000 plus a success fee of 10–15% of the approved amount), rather than "pay upfront" or "100% commission on the approved amount." Fourth, **ask to see past cases.** A consultant should be able to provide at least 5–10 success stories (with the client's written consent), including the subsidy category, approved amount, industry, etc. Finally, **we recommend starting with officially recommended consultants.** Local Chambers of Commerce and Industry and SME support centers maintain lists of partnered, certified consultants who have at least been government-vetted.
