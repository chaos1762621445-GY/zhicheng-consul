# The 10 Most Common Reasons Subsidy Applications Fail—How Many Apply to You?

When Chinese business owners in Japan apply for government subsidies, they are often full of hope, only to end up receiving a notice of rejection ("not selected"). According to data from Japan's SME Agency, about 40–50% of first-time applicants fail. So which parts of the process go wrong? This article takes an in-depth look at why subsidy applications fail and helps you avoid the common application traps.

## Why Is the Subsidy Failure Rate So High?

Competition for Japanese subsidies is fierce, and review standards are strict. Take the Business Innovation Subsidy as an example: the adoption rate in FY2023 was only 35.2%, meaning that more than 6 out of every 10 applications were rejected. The harsher reality is that many applications fail not because the project itself is poor, but because of **poor preparation of the application documents**, **a misunderstanding of the review's focus**, or **muddled logic in the application materials**.

In my many years of consulting practice, I've found that Chinese business owners' reasons for failure have their own peculiarities—involving both issues of language expression and a misunderstanding of the Japanese reviewers' mindset.

## Failure Reason 1: Misjudging Eligibility—Never Truly Knowing Whether You Qualify

### A Common Occurrence

Many applicants only discover at the final stage of submission that their company type or size doesn't meet the subsidy requirements at all. For example, some subsidies clearly define "SME"—for a manufacturing company, capital of no more than JPY 300 million and no more than 300 employees. A Chinese business owner running a trading company in Tokyo had the employees of an affiliated company counted in, bringing the total headcount to 350; at the last moment, the company was judged to be a **large enterprise**, and the entire application was voided.

### The Failure Mechanism

- **Misunderstanding the definition of "capital"**: Many Chinese business owners think "capital" is the same as that year's profit or the cash in their account. In fact, capital refers to the amount injected when the company was founded or increased its capital—a legal concept unrelated to profit.
- **Miscalculating employee headcount**: The employees of affiliated companies and subsidiaries must be combined into the count. Non-regular employees such as dispatched workers and part-timers must be included in the headcount as long as they appear in the company's books.
- **Misclassifying the industry**: Even for the same manufacturing company, filing under "commerce and services" results in a completely different capital ceiling.

### Specific Ways to Avoid the Pitfall

1. **Conduct a compliance check before applying**: Find the "eligibility requirements" section of the subsidy and compare it line by line. It's best to have a Japanese Zeirishi or Gyoseishoshi confirm it.
2. **Prepare your corporation's latest financial statements and articles of incorporation**: These two documents are key to proving your status.
3. **If you have affiliated companies, map out the affiliation chart in advance**: Include shareholding ratios and worker dispatch arrangements to ensure nothing is missed.

A real case: A Chinese manufacturing business owner, with my assistance, discovered in advance that his subsidiary's employees should be counted in. They promptly adjusted the company's structure, finally met the subsidy requirements, and ultimately secured a JPY 6 million subsidy.

## Failure Reason 2: An Unconvincing Business Plan—The "Story" Isn't Strong Enough

### How the Problem Shows Up

The business plan is the soul of a subsidy application. Many Chinese business owners' plans have problems like these:
- Too macro-level, with no specific figures to back them up (e.g., "we will increase sales," without stating specific numbers)
- Weak connection between the project and the company's current situation (Why this project? Why now?)
- Overly optimistic financial projections (predicting sales will double in three years, with no market basis)

### Analysis of the Deeper Cause

Reviewers evaluate hundreds of applications every day, and their review criteria are clear: Is this plan **specific and feasible?** Does the **business owner truly understand what they are doing?** Is there a **realistic probability of success?**

One failed application I saw was written like this:
> "We will achieve digital transformation by introducing a new system."

This sounds fine, but reviewers will ask:
- What system? How much does it cost?
- How much will costs drop after introduction? On what basis?
- Can your employees use it? Is there a training plan?
- Have competitors used this system? With what results?

### Steps to Improve

1. **Make every statement "specific and quantified"**
   - Poor phrasing: We will improve production efficiency
   - Good phrasing: By introducing automated welding machinery, we will lower the product defect rate from 8% to 3%, with an expected cost reduction of JPY 2 million per year

2. **Clarify cause-and-effect and the timeline**
   ```
   Months 1–2: Train employees, deploy the system
   Months 3–6: Trial run, optimize and adjust
   Months 7–12: Full-scale application, evaluate results
   ```

3. **Provide external supporting evidence**
   - Industry reports, market survey data
   - Competitor cases (what results did they get with this approach)
   - Recommendation letters from suppliers or consulting firms

## Failure Reason 3: Not Realizing You Don't Qualify—The "Eligible Company" Requirement Is Overlooked

### Examples of Specific Restrictions

The Business Innovation Subsidy has implicit restrictions on applicants:
- **Tax filing status**: Any unpaid taxes in the previous year means outright rejection
- **Employment insurance enrollment**: If employees are not formally enrolled in employment insurance, you cannot apply
- **Business succession**: If the applying entity is a company founded within three years before applying, many subsidies cannot be applied for
- **Excess liabilities over assets**: If the company's debt ratio is too high (e.g., negative net assets), reviewers will scrutinize feasibility strictly

### Why Are Chinese Businesses Especially Prone to This?

Among Chinese businesses, quite a few adopt a "flexible management" approach:
- Employees exist in the form of dispatched workers, without a formal employment relationship
- For tax optimization, there may be a history of "unpaid taxes" or "thin-profit filings"
- Cross-border e-commerce companies with unclear points of sale and irregular tax filings

These practices may be fine in day-to-day operations, but the moment they touch a government subsidy application, they become fatal wounds.

### Preventive Measures

1. **Do your "subsidy preparation" 3–6 months in advance**
   - Ensure all employees have formal employment contracts and employment insurance
   - Complete the previous year's tax filing (if delayed)
   - Organize financial statements to ensure the data is genuine and compliant

2. **Consult a professional**
   - Before formally applying, have a Zeirishi or Gyoseishoshi conduct a "subsidy compliance diagnosis"
   - The cost is about JPY 50,000–100,000—well worth it compared to the subsidy amount (usually JPY 500,000–10 million)

## Failure Reason 4: Inconsistent Financial Data—The Numbers Lied

### A Problem Case

The data entered in the application doesn't match the financial statements the company submitted:
- The business plan says "sales of JPY 1.5 million per month," but the actual financial statements show average monthly sales of JPY 1 million
- The application states 100 employees, but the staffing table shows only 85
- The plan is to invest JPY 5 million to purchase equipment, but the budget only lists JPY 3 million

When reviewers compare the materials and find these contradictions, **their first reaction is "the applicant is being deceptive."** Even an innocent mistake will be judged as dishonest, leading directly to rejection.

### Why Do Errors Occur So Easily?

1. **The application materials are handled by multiple people**: The manager writes the business plan, the finance department provides the statements, and the two sides don't reconcile their information
2. **Using temporary data**: Extrapolating the annual figure from the most recent month's data, resulting in a large gap from historical data
3. **Exchange-rate issues** (especially for Chinese businesses): If there are overseas sales, the yen-denominated figures may fluctuate

### The Verification and Correction Process

1. **Do a "triple check" before submitting**
   - First check: data self-review (the applicant compares each item themselves)
   - Second check: finance department confirmation (ensuring the statement data is accurate)
   - Third check: professional advisor review (a professional checks from the "reviewer's perspective")

2. **Create a "data comparison table"**
   ```
   Item              Application value   Financial statement value   Notes on discrepancy
   Last year's sales  JPY 18M            JPY 17.5M                    Acceptable within ±JPY 500K
   Employee count     50                 48                           2 in the probation period
   ```

3. **If there are discrepancies, explain them proactively**
   - Don't make the reviewer guess the reason for the discrepancy
   - State it proactively in the application: "Due to reason X, there is a discrepancy Z in data Y"

A real case: An import-export company had a mismatch between the financial data in its application and the fiscal year of its Japanese tax filings, because the fiscal years in China and Japan differ. We helped them recompile the financial statements by the Japanese accounting year, and the problem was readily solved.

## Failure Reason 5: Misunderstanding "Eligible Expenses"—The Money You Want Covered Actually Isn't Covered

### Expense Types Easily Rejected

Many business owners think "as long as I spend money, I can apply for a subsidy," but that is far from the truth. The following expenses, even if incurred, are **not within the subsidy scope**:

| Expense Type | Subsidizable? | Notes |
|---------|--------|------|
| Purchasing machinery and equipment | ✓ Yes | But it must be for business development, not for replacing old equipment |
| Increases in employee wages | ✗ No | Labor costs are generally not covered, unless there is a specific subsidy type |
| Office rent | ✗ No | Lease costs are not eligible under most subsidies |
| Repayment of existing debt | ✗ No | This is the company's own responsibility |
| Advertising and promotion costs | △ Conditionally | Must be directly related to the subsidized project, not general promotion |
| Consulting fees | ✓ Yes | But must be professional consulting related to the subsidy plan |
| R&D expenses | ✓ Yes | Development of new products and new technologies is covered |

### Common Misconceptions Among Chinese Businesses

1. **Treating the "subsidy" as an "interest-free loan"**
   - A subsidy is not a cash loan, but an "after-the-fact reimbursement"
   - You must spend the money yourself first, and only after the project is completed can you apply for reimbursement
   - The expenses must be incurred **within the subsidy period** (usually 6–12 months)

2. **Confusing the "subsidy amount" with the "eligible expenses"**
   - Suppose the subsidy rate is two-thirds
   - If the eligible expenses are JPY 3 million, you can only receive JPY 2 million in subsidy
   - The remaining JPY 1 million must be borne by the company itself
   - Many applicants miscalculate this, causing cash-flow problems

3. **Including non-eligible expenses in the application**
   - For example, the plan mixes in a cost like "employee bonuses"
   - During review, this money is found to be ineligible
   - As a result, **the professionalism of the entire application is called into question**

### Strict Ways to Avoid the Pitfall

1. **Check the "eligible expenses" section of the subsidy requirements item by item**
   - Usually on pages 3–4 of the requirements document
   - Lists all categories of eligible expenses
   - Every one of your budget items should correspond to one of these categories

2. **Create an "expense-category comparison table"**
   ```
   Planned expenditure     Requirement category    Eligible?    Amount
   CNC machinery purchase   Equipment cost          ✓           JPY 5M
   Installation and setup   Equipment-related cost  ✓           JPY 500K
   Employee skills training Labor cost              ✗           JPY 1M (not covered)
   ```

3. **Consult a certified expert**
   - Before submitting, confirm each expense with a certified subsidy advisor
   - Better to spend an extra JPY 50,000 in advisory fees than to lose everything over an expense issue

## Failure Reason 6: Muddled Logic in the Application—The Reviewer Can't Understand What You're Saying

### Common Expression Problems

Japanese subsidy applications demand extremely high logic and clarity. Many Chinese business owners' applications have problems like these:

**Problem Type 1: Unclear hierarchical relationships**
```
Poor writing:
Our company has expertise in artificial intelligence. We plan to introduce an ERP system.
Our employees are of high caliber. This project requires training.
The target market is the Asian region.

Good writing:
(Background) Our current manual bookkeeping method is costly and error-prone, with an average monthly error rate of 5%.
(Goal) By introducing an ERP system, we will reduce bookkeeping costs by 30% and lower the error rate to below 0.5%.
(Measures) Phase 1: purchase the ERP system; Phase 2: train all staff; Phase 3: trial run and optimize.
```

**Problem Type 2: Data leaps, lacking progression**
- "Sales will grow 50%" appears suddenly, but nothing before it explains why it can grow, or on what basis

**Problem Type 3: Stiffness in the Japanese expression**
- Improper word choice, nonstandard use of honorifics, overly long sentence structures
- A heavy "translation accent"—reviewers know at a glance that "this was written by a non-native Japanese speaker"

### A Structured Writing Method

The golden structure of an application is "Background–Current Situation–Challenge–Goal–Measures–Effect":

```
【Background】
We are a precision machining company founded in 2015, with annual sales of JPY 20 million.

【Current Situation / Challenge】
We currently use conventional CNC machines with a machining precision of ±0.1mm and a capacity of 50 units per day on average.
Market demand is upgrading, and products for which customers require precision of ±0.05mm or better have increased by 40%.
If we don't upgrade, we will lose these high-end customers, with an expected drop in annual revenue of JPY 3 million.

【Goal】
By introducing the latest 5-axis simultaneous CNC machine, we will achieve a machining precision of ±0.05mm,
aiming to raise the proportion of high-end customers from 10% to 40% within three years, with sales growth of JPY 5 million.

【Specific Measures】
1. Purchase a new CNC machine (investment of JPY 8 million, of which the eligible amount is JPY 6 million)
2. Employee training (3 existing employees attend a one-month intensive course)
3. Optimize the production flow (with the help of consulting experts, adjust the production process)

【Expected Effects】
- Machining precision improved to ±0.05mm, meeting high-end customers' requirements
- Capacity increased to 80 units per day on average (a 60% increase)
- Expected annual revenue increase of JPY 5 million, with a payback period of 2 years
```

A structure like this is clear and logically rigorous, and reviewers grasp it at a glance.

## Failure Reason 7: Insufficient Judgment of the "Owner's Execution Capability"—Reviewers Assess Whether You Can Really Pull It Off

### The Hidden Evaluation Dimension

In the subsidy review process, there is one important but invisible criterion: **Can this business owner really make this project happen?**

Reviewers judge this through clues like:
- The depth of your understanding of your company's current situation
- Your command of the project's details
- Your past track record of execution
- Whether your decision-making logic is rational

### Behaviors That Easily Trigger a "Lacks Capability" Judgment

1. **Being unable to answer key questions**
   - During review, if asked "After introducing this system, how will your competitors respond?" and you can't answer, it's very dangerous
   - "How much does this equipment cost? What alternatives are available domestically?"—you must be able to answer fluently as well

2. **Insufficient awareness of industry trends**
   - The proposed project runs counter to the industry's direction (e.g., wanting to expand capacity in an already declining industry)
   - Not understanding competitors' movements

3. **The same project has already failed, but with no reflection**
   - "We tried this project three years ago and failed"—if you apply for the same project this time without explaining why it will succeed now, reviewers will be very skeptical

### Ways to Strengthen the "Execution Capability" Impression

1. **Provide a "chain of evidence" to prove your execution ability**
   - Cases of past successful projects (with results as proof)
   - The company's internal project management system
   - The professional qualifications and experience of team members

2. **Detail the timeline and responsibilities**
   ```
   Project management lead: Taro Tanaka (leader), 5 years of CNC operating experience
   Project supervisor: Li Ming (the business owner), ensuring the project advances on schedule
   Progress checked once a month, with immediate adjustments if there is any deviation
   ```

3. **Identify and explain risks**
   - Don't pretend the project has no risks at all
   - Instead, proactively state "the risks we have identified are X, Y, Z, and our countermeasures are..."
   - This will, on the contrary, increase the reviewer's trust

## Failure Reason 8: Subsidy Policy Updates Change Eligibility—Not Keeping Up with the Latest Policy

### The Reality of Policy Changes

Japanese subsidy policies are updated every year. Certain subsidies may:
- Have their subsidy amount reduced
- See their adoption rate fall
- Have new requirements added
- Have a subsidy that existed the previous year abolished

### A Real Case

The FY2023 Business Innovation Subsidy introduced a mandatory "DX (digital transformation)" requirement: even if a project didn't focus on DX, it had to include "digitalization"-related content. Many companies that successfully applied in FY2022 were rejected outright in 2023 when they submitted using the old application—because it didn't meet the new requirements.

### The Information Disadvantage of Chinese Businesses

1. **A single channel for obtaining information**
   - Many Chinese business owners rely on Chinese-language sources like WeChat and Baidu
   - The most authoritative sources for the latest Japanese government subsidy policies are the SME Agency and the official websites of each local government
   - Chinese-language information often lags 2–3 months behind

2. **No "policy monitoring mechanism" in place**
   - You don't know when the policy will change
   - You passively discover "oh, the policy changed" only when applying

### Prevention and Response Measures

1. **Set up policy information monitoring**
   - Follow the SME Agency website (www.chusho.meti.go.jp)
   - Subscribe to subsidy announcements from local governments (prefectural offices, city halls)
   - Regularly (monthly) check whether the subsidies you follow have been updated

2. **Hire an advisor for "policy tracking"**
   - A professional advisor keeps an eye on policy changes at all times
   - If you plan to apply for subsidies on an ongoing basis, spending JPY 30,000–50,000 a year on an advisory subscription is worth it

3. **Reserve time for questions**
   - Most subsidies have a "briefing session" (setsumeikai) where you can ask questions on the spot
   - Attend the briefing and clarify the policy details

## Failure Reason 9: Rushed Preparation of Application Documents—"Working in Fits and Starts" Leads to Insufficient Quality

### A Typical Scheduling Mistake

```
Poor schedule:
- January 1: See the subsidy announcement
- January 15: Start writing the application
- January 25: Submit (only 10 days spent)

Correct schedule:
- January 1: Gather information, do an initial assessment of eligibility
- January 5: Draw up a detailed plan, collect data
- January 10: Complete the first draft, internal review
- January 20: External advisor review, revise
- January 25: Final adjustments, submit
```

### The Specific Consequences of Rushed Preparation

1. **Logical gaps**: Inconsistent statements go unnoticed
2. **Data errors**: Financial data isn't thoroughly checked
3. **Document quality**: Stiff Japanese expression, even grammatical errors
4. **Missing elements**: Some key information isn't provided (e.g., a certain attachment to the financial statements)

### A Realistic Time Plan

How much time does a high-quality subsidy application take from start to finish?

| Stage | Time Required | Task |
|-----|--------|------|
| Information gathering and assessment | 3–5 days | Confirm eligibility, understand requirements, initial feasibility analysis |
| Data preparation | 5–7 days | Collect financial statements, employee rosters, and other materials |
| First draft | 7–10 days | Write the business plan, project content, etc. |
| Internal review and revision | 3–5 days | One round of internal company review |
| Professional advisor review | 5–7 days | Professional review by an external advisor |
| Final revision and preparation | 3–5 days | Adjust, gather supplementary materials, finalize |
| **Total** | **25–40 days** | We recommend allowing 6–8 weeks |

### Recommended Preparation Method

1. **Draw up an application plan immediately**
   - Once the subsidy is announced, don't hesitate—immediately confirm whether you will apply
   - If you decide to apply, draw up a time plan that same day

2. **Advance in phases**
   - Don't wait until 2 weeks before the deadline to start
   - The early stages can be a bit rough, but leave enough time for later revisions

3. **Line up an advisor in advance**
   - If you need external help, don't wait until the application is written to find one
   - Consult early and plan early—an advisor can help you avoid many mistakes

## Failure Reason 10: A Lack of "Effective Communication" with Reviewers—Don't Wait Passively After Applying

### The "Communication Opportunities" in a Subsidy Application

Many applicants think "once I submit the application, I'm done," but that's not the case. During the review process, there are usually the following communication opportunities:

1. **A request for "additional materials" after the initial review**
   - The reviewer may ask you to submit supplementary materials
   - This is a chance to "correct errors" and "add explanations"
   - How effectively you meet this request affects the final result

2. **Interview / hearing (mensetsu / hiaringu)**
   - Some subsidies have an interview stage in the final review
   - This is a chance to explain the project directly to the reviewer
   - Interview performance may account for 20–30% of the final score

3. **A "re-challenge" (reapplication) after rejection**
   - If you are rejected the first time, you can still apply in the next year or the next round
   - Many successful applications actually succeeded only on the 2nd or 3rd try

### Common Communication Shortcomings Among Chinese Businesses

1. **Slow response to requests for additional materials**
   - After receiving the request, delaying for several days before preparing
   - Not understanding what the reviewer really wants, and submitting irrelevant materials

2. **Insufficient interview preparation**
   - Last-minute cramming, unable to clearly explain the project details
   - Improper word choice, leaving the reviewer with the impression that "the business owner hasn't even figured it out themselves"

3. **Giving up after rejection**
   - Thinking "since I was rejected, I can never apply again" (a mistaken idea)
   - Not analyzing the reasons for failure, and simply reapplying with the same content

### Improvement Strategies

1. **Set up a "prompt response" mechanism**
   - Designate one person responsible for communication with the review department
   - Respond within 24 hours to any email from the review department
   - If you don't understand it, consult your advisor immediately—don't guess

2. **Prepare well for the interview**
   ```
   Preparation checklist:
   ☐ The project's complete timetable (monthly progress)
   ☐ The sources and calculation methods for key data
   ☐ Competitive analysis (why we can do better than rivals)
   ☐ Risk identification and countermeasures
   ☐ A detailed calculation of the return on investment
   ```

3. **Analyze the reasons for failure and prepare for a "re-challenge"**
   - Consult the review department (some places offer this service): "Why were we rejected this time?"
   - Revise the plan based on the feedback
   - Study this year's policy to see whether there is a new subsidy better suited to you

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Q1: I've already been rejected (not selected) for one subsidy. Can I still apply for other subsidies?

**A:** Absolutely. Being rejected for one subsidy does not affect your application for others. In fact, Japan has hundreds of subsidies, from national-level ones (the SME Agency, various ministries) to local government ones, each with its own review criteria. It is very common for the same company to be rejected by Subsidy A yet selected by Subsidy B. The recommended approach is: (1) analyze why you were rejected; (2) see whether another subsidy is better suited to your project; (3) you can also reapply for the same subsidy in the next year, but make improvements based on the reasons for failure. Based on my actual cases, about 30% of companies ultimately succeed after their 2nd or 3rd application.

### Q2: If the subsidy ultimately isn't
